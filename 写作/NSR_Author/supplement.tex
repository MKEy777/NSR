\section{Supplementary Methods}\label{sec:supp_methods}

\subsection{Dataset Description}\label{supp:dataset}

To evaluate the framework against prior studies, we tested it on five standard EEG datasets for emotion recognition: SEED \cite{ref65}, SEED-IV \cite{ref48}, SEED-V \cite{ref49}, DEAP \cite{ref50}, and DREAMER \cite{ref51}.

The SEED series from the BCMI laboratory at Shanghai Jiao Tong University allows performance assessment under different label granularities. We used the 62-channel EEG recordings collected during emotion elicitation via film clips. The SEED dataset includes 15 subjects across three sessions and assigns three labels: positive, neutral, and negative. SEED-IV follows an identical protocol of 15 subjects and three sessions but uses four labels (happy, sad, fear, and neutral) across 24 trials per session. SEED-V scales the experiment to 20 subjects and three sessions with 15 trials per session, using five labels: happy, sad, fear, disgust, and neutral.

The DEAP dataset comprises 32 subjects who watched 40 emotion-eliciting music videos. Ratings were reported using the Self-Assessment Manikin on a scale from 1 to 9. The EEG signals were recorded across 32 channels at 512~Hz and subsequently downsampled to 128~Hz. The DREAMER dataset includes 23 subjects and records 14 channels of EEG at 128~Hz via a wearable device. Subjects watched 18 video clips and provided ratings on a scale from 1 to 5. For both datasets, we defined a four-class classification task by combining high and low valence with high and low arousal. Continuous ratings were discretized using a threshold at the midpoint of the rating scale.

We preprocessed the EEG recordings using Z-score normalization, non-overlapping outer-window segmentation, Power Spectral Entropy (PSE) extraction \cite{PSE}, spatial mapping, and Linear Dynamical System (LDS) smoothing \cite{ref65}. Table~\ref{tab:preprocessing_params} distinguishes the outer-window duration and stride from the temporal-bin length used to calculate PSE.

Each EEG channel was first Z-score normalized. The SEED-family recordings were segmented into 4-s outer windows with a 4-s stride; each outer window was represented by four contiguous 1-s PSE bins. DEAP used 9-s outer windows with a 9-s stride and six contiguous 1.5-s PSE bins, whereas DREAMER used 9-s outer windows with a 9-s stride and nine contiguous 1-s PSE bins. Thus, the 1-s and 1.5-s quantities specify PSE temporal bins rather than the stride between outer windows.

For spatial mapping, the PSE values from the EEG electrodes were projected onto an $H \times W$ grid according to the electrode montage, with empty grid locations set to zero. Stacking the $N_f$ temporally contiguous PSE maps yielded one outer-window tensor in $\mathbb{R}^{N_f \times H \times W}$.

Each outer window retained its \texttt{subject\_id}, \texttt{session\_id}, and \texttt{trial\_id}. For SEED, SEED-IV, and SEED-V, \texttt{session\_id} ranged from 1 to 3 and \texttt{trial\_id} denoted the trial ordinal within a session. DEAP and DREAMER were treated as single-session datasets with \texttt{session\_id}=0, and \texttt{trial\_id} denoted the trial ordinal within a subject. A trial was therefore identified by the composite key $(\texttt{subject\_id},\texttt{session\_id},\texttt{trial\_id})$.

After PSE extraction and spatial mapping, the temporally ordered outer windows from each trial formed a sequence $\mathbf{X}^{(q)} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times N_f \times H \times W}$, where $M$ is the number of outer windows in trial $q$. LDS smoothing was applied separately to each trial by smoothing the length-$M$ sequence at every fixed frequency--spatial coordinate $(f,h,w)$. No smoothing was performed across trial, session, or subject boundaries, and a trial containing only one outer window was left unchanged. LDS was applied to the complete trial sequence before the reported data splits were instantiated. Consequently, under the subject-dependent random window-level split used for the main benchmark, different windows from the same trial can occur in both the training and held-out evaluation partitions after having participated in the same trial-wise smoothing sequence; the subject-independent protocols described below instead exclude all windows of each held-out subject from training-set construction and gradient-based parameter updates.

\textbf{End-to-end data transformation pipeline.}
The preprocessing and inference chain can be summarized as
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{ch}} \times f_s \times T_{\mathrm{trial}}}}_{\text{Raw EEG}}
\;\xrightarrow{\text{Z-score and outer windows}}\;
\underbrace{\mathbb{R}^{M \times N_f \times N_{\mathrm{ch}}}}_{\text{Trial-wise PSE sequence}}
\;\xrightarrow{\text{Spatial map}}\;
\underbrace{\mathbb{R}^{M \times N_f \times H \times W}}_{\text{Topographic sequence}}
\;\xrightarrow{\text{trial-wise LDS}}\;
\underbrace{\mathbb{R}^{M \times N_f \times H \times W}}_{\text{Smoothed sequence}}.
\label{eq:preprocessing_pipeline}
\end{equation}
Each smoothed outer-window tensor is subsequently processed by DSGM, encoded into sparse spike times by DF-TTFS, and classified by ATSNN.

\textbf{Composite DSGM tensor dimensions and broadcasting.}
For implementation-level shape bookkeeping, a batch of smoothed outer-window tensors is denoted by $X_0\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times N_f\times H\times W}$, where $B$ is the batch size, $N_f$ is the number of temporally contiguous PSE maps, and $H\times W$ is the electrode-topographic grid. EEG electrodes therefore occupy spatial grid locations rather than forming the $N_f$ axis. The composite DSGM formulation in the main text is retained; the intermediate symbol $U$ is introduced here only to state the implemented dimensions and broadcasting rules explicitly.

The depthwise-separable feature transformation uses a $3\times3$ depthwise convolution with stride 2, padding 1, and $N_f$ groups, followed by a $1\times1$ pointwise convolution that maps $N_f$ input channels to $C_o=2N_f$ output channels. Its output is
\begin{equation}
U\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times C_o\times H'\times W'},\qquad
H'=\left\lfloor\frac{H+2p-k}{s}\right\rfloor+1=\left\lceil\frac{H}{2}\right\rceil,\quad
W'=\left\lceil\frac{W}{2}\right\rceil,
\label{eq:dsgm_aligned_feature}
\end{equation}
for $k=3$, $s=2$, and $p=1$. Thus, the half-size labels in Main Fig.~2 indicate stride-2 downsampling schematically; the exact integer dimensions follow Eq.~\ref{eq:dsgm_aligned_feature}, including when $H$ or $W$ is odd.

\subsection{Controlled EEG Perturbation Protocol}\label{supp:controlled_perturbations}

We evaluated sensitivity to synthetic corruption on SEED, DEAP, and DREAMER. Perturbations were applied to the Z-score-normalized, segmented time-domain EEG samples after dataset-specific signal conditioning but before PSE extraction, spatial mapping, and LDS smoothing. The perturbation stage therefore altered the signal samples from which all downstream features were recomputed; no perturbation was added directly to the PSE feature maps. The sampling rate passed to the generator was 200~Hz for SEED and 128~Hz for DEAP and DREAMER.

For each dataset, channel $c$, and recording unit, the clean-signal standard deviation $\sigma_c$ was estimated over the available time-domain samples. The relative noise level was defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NL}=\frac{\sigma_{n,c}}{\sigma_c},
\label{eq:relative_noise_level}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{n,c}$ is the standard deviation of the generated perturbation after channel-wise normalization. We evaluated $\mathrm{NL}\in\{0.01,0.03,0.05,0.08,0.10,0.125\}$ together with the unperturbed condition (Clean, NL$=0$).

Three perturbation families were generated. Gaussian corruption consisted of independent zero-mean white noise scaled to $\mathrm{NL}\,\sigma_c$. Low-frequency drift was produced by first-order Butterworth low-pass filtering of a session-scale white-noise trace with a 0.5-Hz cutoff; the resulting trace was normalized per channel before scaling to the target NL. EMG-like corruption consisted of Poisson-triggered transient bursts generated continuously along the recording time axis. The generator used an average event rate of 0.5 bursts per second per channel and Gaussian envelopes with full width at half maximum sampled uniformly from 20 to 50~ms. Carrier frequencies and all conversions from seconds to samples were passed according to the dataset sampling rate, with each carrier band constrained below the corresponding Nyquist frequency. Each EMG-like trace was root-mean-square normalized per channel and then scaled to $\mathrm{NL}\,\sigma_c$.

For reproducibility, a deterministic noise seed was derived for each dataset recording, perturbation type, and NL. DA-SNN, DH-SNN, and EEGNet used the same generated feature bundle, random window-level 80/20 split, model seed, training schedule, checkpoint-selection rule, and evaluation level within each condition. A separate noise-matched training and model-selection run was conducted for every perturbation type and NL; the analysis did not apply a single clean-trained checkpoint to all noisy test sets. The reported trajectories are from one model seed and are therefore summarized as individual accuracies and descriptive clean-to-NL$=0.125$ differences, without error bars or significance tests.

\textbf{Subject-dependent main benchmark.}
For Main Table~1, the split unit was the outer window. Approximately 80\% of the pooled windows were assigned to training and 20\% to a held-out evaluation partition using class-stratified random splitting. This protocol is subject-dependent and subject-overlapping: windows from the same subject, and potentially from the same trial, can occur on both sides of the split. It is used to compare the 15 models under a common benchmark setting and is not interpreted as unseen-subject or unseen-trial generalization.

\textbf{Subject-independent supplementary evaluations.}
For SEED, SEED-IV, and SEED-V, leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) evaluation held out one complete subject per split; all sessions, trials, and windows from that subject were excluded from training-set construction and gradient-based parameter updates. For DEAP and DREAMER, we used five predefined cross-subject subject-holdout splits. Each DEAP split contained 26 training subjects and 6 held-out subjects, and each DREAMER split contained 19 training subjects and 4 held-out subjects. These are fixed subject holdouts with an approximately 80/20 subject ratio, not within-subject 80/20 splits and not standard mutually exclusive fivefold cross-validation.

\textbf{Checkpoint selection.}
No separate validation subset was defined in any of the reported EEG protocols. For each predefined split, the held-out evaluation partition was also evaluated after every epoch and used for checkpoint selection and early stopping based on accuracy; the selected checkpoint was then used to calculate the reported metrics on that same partition. Neural models in the unified reevaluation followed the same split definitions and checkpoint-selection rule. In the subject-independent evaluations, all data from held-out subjects remained excluded from training-set construction and gradient-based parameter updates, although the held-out partition was used for checkpoint selection as stated above. Because model selection and final reporting use the same held-out partition, these results should be interpreted as within-protocol model comparisons rather than estimates from an untouched independent test set.

\textbf{Metrics and aggregation.}
Accuracy is the proportion of correctly classified held-out windows. Macro-F1 is the unweighted mean of the per-class F1 scores. Macro-specificity is computed by treating each class in a one-versus-rest manner, calculating $\mathrm{TN}/(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})$ for each class, and then taking the unweighted mean across classes. When repeat- or split-level measurements were available, we report the arithmetic mean and sample standard deviation (\texttt{ddof}=1). Main Table~1 reports DA-SNN as mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation across repeated runs, while baseline point estimates are retained as available. The SEED-family LOSO results are reported as mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation across held-out-subject splits. The available DEAP and DREAMER cross-subject records contain only aggregate point estimates over the five predefined subject-holdout splits; no dispersion values are imputed.

\section{Supplementary Notes}

\subsection{B1-model Definition and Forward Semantics}

ATSNN adopts the B1-model introduced by Stanojevi\'{c} et~al. \cite{ref46} as a neuron-level spike-time substrate. In the general formulation of that work, the identity-mapping case is specified by $A_i^{(n)}=0$ and $B_i^{(n)}=1$ for every neuron $i$ and layer $n$. The latter fixes the threshold-crossing slope to unity. Each hidden neuron emits at most one spike in the half-open layer window $[T_{\min}^{(n)},T_{\max}^{(n)})$, and adjacent windows satisfy the cascade condition $T_{\min}^{(n)}=T_{\max}^{(n-1)}$. We assume $\epsilon>0$, $T_{\max}^{(n)}>T_{\min}^{(n)}$, and weights and thresholds parameterized so that an active neuron's theoretical crossing lies within its assigned window. Here $\vartheta_i^{(n)}$ denotes the firing threshold in normalized membrane-potential units, and $\epsilon\vartheta_i^{(n)}$ is the corresponding temporal offset.

Solving the constant-slope dynamics gives the theoretical first-passage time
\begin{equation}
\tilde{T}_i^{(n)} = T_{\min}^{(n)} + \epsilon\vartheta_i^{(n)} + \sum_j W_{ij}^{(n)}\!\left(T_j^{(n-1)} - T_{\min}^{(n)}\right).
\label{eq:supp_theoretical_time}
\end{equation}
This crossing becomes an active spike only if it occurs strictly before the upper boundary:
\begin{equation}
M_i^{(n)} = \mathbf{1}\!\left[\tilde{T}_i^{(n)} < T_{\max}^{(n)}\right].
\label{eq:b1_mask}
\end{equation}
The observed, censored time is
\begin{equation}
T_i^{(n)} = M_i^{(n)}\tilde{T}_i^{(n)} + \bigl(1-M_i^{(n)}\bigr)T_{\max}^{(n)}.
\label{eq:supp_actual_time}
\end{equation}
Thus, $M_i^{(n)}=1$ denotes an active neuron, whereas $M_i^{(n)}=0$ denotes an inactive neuron whose stored time is the boundary placeholder. Equality at $T_{\max}^{(n)}$ is assigned to the inactive case. The mask is used when identifying valid events and updating temporal windows. In the forward map, the cascade condition also makes the inactive placeholder equal to the next layer's reference time, so its temporal offset is exactly zero.

For the original B1 identity parameterization, the derivative of the theoretical crossing time with respect to its weighted temporal input $I_i^{(n)}$ is constant,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \tilde{T}_i^{(n)}}{\partial I_i^{(n)}}=1,
\qquad
I_i^{(n)}=\sum_j W_{ij}^{(n)}\!\left(T_j^{(n-1)}-T_{\min}^{(n)}\right),
\label{eq:b1_slope}
\end{equation}
and the latency $\tau_i^{(n)}=T_{\max}^{(n)}-T_i^{(n)}$ can be mapped to a ReLU activation under the weight, threshold, and window assumptions of Ref.~\cite{ref46}:
\begin{equation}
\tau_i^{(n)}=\mathrm{ReLU}\!\left(\sum_j W_{ij}^{(n)}\tau_j^{(n-1)}+b_i^{(n)}\right).
\label{eq:b1_relu}
\end{equation}
This correspondence motivates the selection of B1 as a tractable, hardware-compatible substrate.

The original B1 work also adapts $T_{\max}$ during training through a conditional, upper-bound-oriented rule designed to maintain a margin between active spikes and the window boundary. ATSNN does not claim the general concept of an adaptive temporal window as new. Instead, it uses the midpoint-referenced signed-error rule in Eq.~(\ref{eq:supp_window_update}), which can contract or expand the window according to the observed earliest valid spike. The broader DA-SNN contribution is the integration of this regulation with EEG-specific DSGM processing, power-of-two DF-TTFS encoding, and the GALS accelerator.

\subsection{Division-Free TTFS Encoding Derivation}

TTFS coding maps higher activation values to earlier spike times, generating at most one spike per neuron within a given inference cycle. Standard TTFS encoding normalizes input activations using floating-point division:  
\begin{equation}
V_{\mathrm{norm}} = \frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}-V_{\min}}{V_{\max}-V_{\min}}
\end{equation}
where $V_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the input activation, and $V_{\min}$ and $V_{\max}$ denote the activation bounds. Because floating-point division introduces computational overhead, the proposed division-free encoder replaces it with power-of-two scaling. 

During training, the activation range $(V_{\max}-V_{\min})$ is monitored to define a scaling factor $S_p$ as the smallest power of two bounding this range:
\begin{equation}
S_p = 2^{\lceil \log_2(\max(V_{\max}-V_{\min},\,\delta)) \rceil},
\end{equation}
where $\delta=10^{-5}$ in the float32 training implementation. The exponent $\lceil\log_2(\cdot)\rceil$ is retained as an integer shift count, so $S_p$ is implemented by a power-of-two shift in fixed-point inference. If $V_{\max}=V_{\min}$, the safeguard keeps $S_p$ positive; all normalized activations are then zero and map to the boundary code $T_{\mathrm{spike}}=T_{\max}$ rather than causing division by zero.

After training, parameters $V_{\min}$ and $S_p$ are fixed to ensure deterministic encoding during inference. The normalized activation then becomes
\begin{equation}
V_{\mathrm{norm}} = \mathrm{clip}\left(\frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}-V_{\min}}{S_p},0,1\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{clip}(\cdot)$ constrains the value to the interval $[0,1]$. This normalized value is mapped to a spike time within the temporal window:
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{spike}} = T_{\max}-\mathrm{round}\left(V_{\mathrm{norm}}(T_{\max}-T_{\min})\right).
\end{equation}

This formulation preserves the fundamental property of TTFS coding, where stronger activations correspond to earlier spikes, while maintaining the sparse temporal representations suitable for spiking neural networks. At the hardware level, a threshold filter discards spike events with latencies exceeding the predefined time window before serializing the remaining valid events into address-event representation (AER) packets, thereby minimizing bus traffic.

\subsection{Local Weight-Gradient Relation and Adaptive Temporal-Window Regulation}

\textbf{Forward-map convention.}
The analysis below uses the theoretical crossing time, active mask, and censored observed time defined in Eqs.~(\ref{eq:supp_theoretical_time})--(\ref{eq:supp_actual_time}). An active neuron has $M_i^{(n)}=1$ and $T_i^{(n)}=\tilde{T}_i^{(n)}$; an inactive neuron has $M_i^{(n)}=0$ and stores the boundary placeholder $T_{\max}^{(n)}$.

\textbf{Proposition 1 (Weight gradient).}
For neuron $i$ in layer $n$, the gradient of the loss function $L$ with respect to synaptic weight $W_{ij}^{(n)}$ is given by the chain rule:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{ij}^{(n)}} =
\frac{\partial L}{\partial T_i^{(n)}}
\cdot
\frac{\partial T_i^{(n)}}{\partial W_{ij}^{(n)}}.
\label{eq:supp_chain_rule}
\end{equation}

For active neurons ($T_i^{(n)}=\tilde{T}_i^{(n)}$), differentiating Eq.~(\ref{eq:supp_theoretical_time}) yields
\[
\frac{\partial T_i^{(n)}}{\partial W_{ij}^{(n)}} =
T_j^{(n-1)} - T_{\min}^{(n)}.
\]

For silent neurons, $T_i^{(n)}$ is constant at $T_{\max}^{(n)}$, rendering the gradient zero almost everywhere. Thus, the weight gradient follows the piecewise form

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{ij}^{(n)}} =
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle
\frac{\partial L}{\partial T_i^{(n)}}
\left(
T_j^{(n-1)} - T_{\min}^{(n)}
\right),
& \tilde{T}_i^{(n)} < T_{\max}^{(n)} \\[1.0ex]
0,
& \tilde{T}_i^{(n)} \ge T_{\max}^{(n)}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:supp_gradient_piecewise}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Definition 2 (Temporal offset).}
The presynaptic temporal offset is defined as
\[
\Delta T_{ij}^{(n)} = T_j^{(n-1)} - T_{\min}^{(n)}.
\]

For active neurons, the gradient magnitude is

\[
\left|
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{ij}^{(n)}}
\right|
=
\left|
\frac{\partial L}{\partial T_i^{(n)}}
\right|
\cdot
|\Delta T_{ij}^{(n)}|.
\]

\textbf{Adaptive window regulation.}
Let the valid-spike set be $\mathcal{V}^{(n)}=\{T_i^{(n)}\mid M_i^{(n)}=1\}$. When $\mathcal{V}^{(n)}\neq\emptyset$, its earliest valid spike is
\[
T_e^{(n)}=\min \mathcal{V}^{(n)}.
\]
The window update rule is

\begin{equation}
T_{\max}^{(n)\prime} =
T_{\max}^{(n)}
+
\lambda
\left(
T_e^{(n)} -
\frac{T_{\max}^{(n)}+T_{\min}^{(n)}}{2}
\right).
\label{eq:supp_window_update}
\end{equation}
If $\mathcal{V}^{(n)}=\emptyset$, no $T_e^{(n)}$ is formed and the fallback is $T_{\max}^{(n)\prime}=T_{\max}^{(n)}$. After an update, a minimum width $\delta_T=10^{-4}$ is enforced so that $T_{\max}^{(n)\prime}\ge T_{\min}^{(n)}+\delta_T$. This mechanism contracts the window when valid spikes occur before the midpoint and expands it when they occur after the midpoint.

The complete procedure of this mechanism is detailed in Algorithm~\ref{alg:atsnn_update}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Forward Pass and Adaptive Temporal Window Update}
\label{alg:atsnn_update}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $T^{(n-1)},W^{(n)},\vartheta^{(n)},\epsilon,\lambda,T_{\min}^{(n)},T_{\max}^{(n)},\delta_T,\mathrm{mode}$
\Ensure $T^{(n)},M^{(n)},T_{\max}^{(n)\prime}$
\State $T_{\min}^{(n)}\gets T_{\max}^{(n-1)}$ \Comment{Cascade boundary}
\State $T_{\max}^{(n)\prime}\gets T_{\max}^{(n)}$ \Comment{Default and empty-set fallback}
\Statex \textit{Phase 1: Censored B1-model forward pass}
\For{each neuron $i$ in layer $n$}
    \State $S_i\gets\sum_j W_{ij}^{(n)}\bigl(T_j^{(n-1)}-T_{\min}^{(n)}\bigr)$
    \State $\tilde{T}_i^{(n)}\gets T_{\min}^{(n)}+\epsilon\vartheta_i^{(n)}+S_i$
    \State $M_i^{(n)}\gets\mathbf{1}[\tilde{T}_i^{(n)}<T_{\max}^{(n)}]$
    \State $T_i^{(n)}\gets M_i^{(n)}\tilde{T}_i^{(n)}+(1-M_i^{(n)})T_{\max}^{(n)}$
\EndFor
\Statex \textit{Phase 2: Midpoint-referenced window update}
\If{$\mathrm{mode}=\texttt{train}$}
    \State $\mathcal{V}^{(n)}\gets\{T_i^{(n)}\mid M_i^{(n)}=1\}$
    \If{$\mathcal{V}^{(n)}\neq\emptyset$}
        \State $T_e^{(n)}\gets\min\mathcal{V}^{(n)}$
        \State $\bar{T}_{\max}\gets T_{\max}^{(n)}+\lambda\left(T_e^{(n)}-(T_{\max}^{(n)}+T_{\min}^{(n)})/2\right)$
        \State $T_{\max}^{(n)\prime}\gets\max\bigl(\bar{T}_{\max},T_{\min}^{(n)}+\delta_T\bigr)$
    \EndIf
\EndIf
\State $T_{\min}^{(n+1)}\gets T_{\max}^{(n)\prime}$
\State \Return $T^{(n)},M^{(n)},T_{\max}^{(n)\prime}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Continuous output readout and inactive-neuron semantics.}
The final layer is nonspiking and integrates the latency-coded hidden representation into real-valued class logits. Because $T_{\min}^{(L)}=T_{\max}^{(L-1)}$, an inactive hidden neuron stores $T_j^{(L-1)}=T_{\max}^{(L-1)}=T_{\min}^{(L)}$ and contributes exactly zero. Therefore, the implemented observed-time readout is algebraically identical to an explicitly masked readout:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
z_k
&=\sum_j W_{kj}^{(L)}\!\left(T_{\min}^{(L)}-T_j^{(L-1)}\right)+\epsilon\vartheta_k^{(L)}\\
&=\sum_j W_{kj}^{(L)}M_j^{(L-1)}\!\left(T_{\min}^{(L)}-\tilde{T}_j^{(L-1)}\right)+\epsilon\vartheta_k^{(L)}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:masked_logit}
\end{equation}
The scalar boundary placeholder is thus never interpreted as a valid late spike. The explicit mask remains necessary for event validity, window adaptation, and any analysis that distinguishes active from censored neurons.

\subsection{Quantization Strategy}
We represent the model with signed INT8 weights and unsigned 8-bit nonnegative activations during inference to avoid floating-point computation. This choice reduces the data volume of weights and activations and suits FPGA execution. Uniform per-tensor 8-bit quantization is used: each quantized weight or activation tensor has its own scale, rather than sharing one scale across the complete network. We use symmetric scaling for weights and a zero-valued offset for nonnegative activations. For a real-valued weight tensor $r$, the scale $s_w$ and signed quantizer $Q_s(\cdot)$ are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s_w &= \frac{\max\!\left(\max(|r|),\epsilon_q\right)}{127},\\
Q_s(r) &= \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{r}{s_w}\right),-127,127\right),
\qquad \hat{r}=s_w Q_s(r),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:quant_w}
\end{equation}

where $\epsilon_q>0$ prevents division by zero for an all-zero tensor. The factor 127 maps the positive and negative extrema to $+127$ and $-127$, respectively, without clipping-induced asymmetry; the additional INT8 code $-128$ is left unused.

For a nonnegative activation tensor $r$, the unsigned scale $s_a$ and quantizer $Q_u(\cdot)$ are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s_a &= \frac{\max\!\left(\max(r),\epsilon_q\right)}{255},\\
Q_u(r) &= \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{r}{s_a}\right),0,255\right),
\qquad \hat{r}=s_a Q_u(r),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:quant_a}
\end{equation}
where the activation maximum used to determine $s_a$ is obtained from the complete SEED training partition used by the ordinary random window-level 80/20 training protocol. No held-out test sample is used for calibration, and the resulting per-tensor activation scale is fixed for inference. Both quantizers use round-to-nearest followed by saturation to their stated integer ranges.

To mitigate accuracy loss, we apply quantization-aware training rather than post-training quantization. Starting from the converged floating-point model, training resumes with a smaller learning rate while $Q_s(\cdot)$ and $Q_u(\cdot)$ are applied in the forward pass; after each update, weights are projected back onto the quantized set \cite{ref26}. Batch Normalization (BN) folding is performed offline by merging each BN transformation into the weights and biases of the preceding convolution before integer inference.



\subsection{Detailed Hardware Control Protocol}
The accelerator follows a globally asynchronous, locally synchronous (GALS) organization with two independent local clock islands. The input/feature buffers, tensor compute engine (TCE), multi-branch fusion path, and division-free TTFS encoder operate in the \texttt{clk\_TCE} island. The lower weight memory, compute-unit/processing-element (CU/PE) arrays, spike collector, and final-layer state operate in the \texttt{clk\_SNN} island; the downstream comparator-tree decision unit consumes the final 32-bit membrane potentials. These islands do not share a chip-wide execution clock. The AER block is a composite boundary: packet formation and Req generation occur on the sending side, clock-domain-crossing (CDC) synchronization occurs at the boundary, and routing, broadcasting, and hidden-layer recirculation occur on the \texttt{clk\_SNN} side.

Event transfer uses a bundled-data four-phase protocol. When DF-TTFS generates a valid spike time $t_i$, the sending interface places the address and timestamp on the payload bus, allows the bus to stabilize, and asserts request (Req). The payload remains unchanged throughout the Req--Ack transaction. Req passes through a two-stage synchronizer into the \texttt{clk\_SNN} island; after the synchronized request is detected, the receiver samples the stable payload, decodes its destination from the current layer/controller context, enables the required CU/PE array, and schedules the membrane-potential update. When all target updates for the event are complete, the receiver asserts acknowledgment (Ack), which passes through a two-stage synchronizer back into the \texttt{clk\_TCE} island.

\centering

\caption{Timing diagram of the bundled-data four-phase handshake between the locally synchronous \texttt{clk\_TCE} and \texttt{clk\_SNN} islands. The address/timestamp payload is stabilized before request (Req) assertion and held until the synchronized acknowledgment (Ack) completes the transfer.}
\label{fig:handshake_timing}
\end{figure}

As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:handshake_timing}, the transfer proceeds in four control transitions: the sender asserts Req after stabilizing the payload; the receiver observes synchronized Req and asserts Ack after accepting and processing the event; the sender observes synchronized Ack and deasserts Req; and the receiver observes synchronized Req low and deasserts Ack. Only after synchronized Ack returns low may the sender release or replace the payload and begin the next transfer. The multi-bit address/timestamp bus is therefore not synchronized bit by bit. Data consistency instead follows the bundled-data stability rule, with the synchronized single-bit control signals defining the sampling and release window.

The stall behavior provides backpressure: while a transfer is outstanding, the sender cannot advance to or overwrite the next event. No dual-clock FIFO is used on the AER/CDC path; the FIFO shown in main-text Fig.~4(b) belongs only to the TCE line buffer. The handshake therefore protects the in-flight bundled payload rather than preventing overflow of an unimplemented event FIFO.

The implementation uses register clock enables controlled by local finite-state machines; it does not rely on a confirmed BUFGCE or other dedicated clock-gating primitive. In the \texttt{clk\_TCE} island, the controller schedules the main, channel-gating, and spatial-gating paths and clears the clock enable of a completed path while it waits for the remaining paths. In the \texttt{clk\_SNN} island, layer/array enables select the required time-multiplexed compute resources, while inactive registers and datapaths retain state with their local clock enables deasserted.

The single GALS Flow Controller coordinates start/enable, completion, and layer/array-enable state. Idle is a local finite-state-machine state after completion, and backpressure is the Req/Ack stall behavior rather than a separate controller port. The controller does not carry tensor or spike payloads and does not distribute a shared execution clock between the two islands.

\subsubsection*{GALS boundary and clock-domain considerations.}
Both compute regions are locally synchronous: TCE-side state is updated by \texttt{clk\_TCE}, and SNN-side state is updated by \texttt{clk\_SNN}. The two-stage synchronizers are applied to the single-bit Req and Ack controls in their respective receiving domains; they are not applied independently to each payload bit. This structure reduces CDC metastability risk, but no numerical mean-time-between-failures claim is made because the required device parameters and timing report are not included in the present analysis.

\subsubsection*{Sources of energy efficiency.}
The architecture exposes three distinct efficiency mechanisms. First, \emph{model-induced event sparsity}: TTFS generates at most one spike per neuron per inference cycle, and censored neurons generate no event, reducing AER transfers, weight reads, and membrane updates. Second, \emph{event-triggered execution and data movement}: routing, local memory access, and CU/PE updates occur only for accepted events. Third, \emph{local clock-enable control}: completed or inactive datapaths retain state rather than performing unnecessary clocked updates. The last mechanism reduces dynamic switching activity; it is not claimed to reduce static power. The matched synchronous comparison reported below evaluates the net implementation-level trade-off, but it does not establish that total dynamic power is proportional only to event rate.

\subsubsection*{GALS interface overhead.}
The GALS interface has non-zero cost. Its implementation includes event-valid filtering, AER routing/broadcast and hidden-layer recirculation, address decoding, the forward and return two-stage synchronizers, the four-phase handshake state machines, and clock-enable control. These components consume logic and add transfer latency, and each accepted event incurs routing and protocol work even when the event stream is sparse. Their LUT/flip-flop counts and functional latencies are resolved from the top-level hierarchical synthesis and reported below. Component-level dynamic power is not separately resolved; no unmeasured percentage or negligible-overhead claim is therefore assigned to individual interface components.

\subsection{Streaming Line Buffer for Convolution}
To sustain high-throughput convolution under streaming input conditions, the tensor compute engine uses a line buffer constructed from shift registers to reconstruct sliding convolution windows on-chip \cite{conv}.

The line buffer acts as a cascade of FIFO registers storing recent input samples from the data stream. When a new EEG sample enters the pipeline, the buffer updates its internal state via column shifting. This process reconstructs the spatial context required for convolution without repeatedly accessing external memory. For a $3\times3$ convolution kernel, the buffer maintains three rows of feature data, continuously forming local windows by combining the current input with stored samples. The resulting sliding window is forwarded to the multiply-accumulate pipeline every clock cycle.

This reconstruction converts a serialized data stream into parallel convolution windows, minimizing off-chip memory bandwidth and maintaining continuous pipeline utilization. During multibranch execution, concurrent access from the main branch, channel gate, and spatial gate exceeds the dual-port limit of standard block RAMs (BRAMs), creating structural hazards. The architecture therefore implements a multibank buffer supporting one write stream and three read streams per pipeline stage. By banking BRAMs with replication and scheduled reads, the buffer virtualizes multiport access. This design keeps the external interface free of stalls and ensures that internal storage conflicts do not limit feature extraction throughput.

\subsection{Hardware Realization of Batch Normalization and Hard-Sigmoid}
To reduce pipeline depth, the output stage integrates logic that performs batch normalization, quantization, and activation within a single datapath. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:BN_RELU_HSigmoid}(a), we use the linearity of convolution to apply an offline parameter folding strategy, merging normalization parameters into the preceding convolution weights and biases. For the Hard-Sigmoid function in the gating paths, we implement a circuit using shift, add, and clamp operations (Fig.~\ref{fig:BN_RELU_HSigmoid}(b)) to avoid exponential calculations. The input is shifted right by three bits and biased by a fixed-point constant (0.5), followed by clamping via comparators.

\subsection{Event-Driven Processing Element Microarchitecture}
Each SNN processing element (PE) follows an event-driven execution model controlled by a local finite-state machine (FSM) in the \texttt{clk\_SNN} island. The FSM schedules accepted events and uses a register clock enable to hold inactive state during idle periods.

By default, the PE is idle and its state-update clock enable is deasserted. When the synchronized AER request is accepted and the array is selected, the FSM asserts the enable and activates the datapath. During computation, the PE performs a time-modulated multiply--accumulate operation. The datapath subtracts a local reference time from the incoming spike timestamp to obtain the temporal difference. This difference is multiplied by the synaptic weight and accumulated into a 32-bit membrane-potential register.

After computation, the PE asserts a local completion status and enters a wait state. The array-level barrier aggregates completion from the active PEs; the AER receiver asserts Ack only after the event update is complete. The PE then returns to idle and deasserts its clock enable. This mechanism avoids unnecessary state updates when no valid event is being processed, without implying that the local clock network is physically disconnected.


\subsection{Memory-Centric SNN Array Organization}

To address the frequent weight accesses inherent in spiking neural networks, the compute fabric uses a memory-centric architecture where weight storage is co-located with computation. Each SNN array integrates a dedicated SRAM interface that supplies a 32-bit weight word per read cycle. Inside the array, this word is unpacked into four INT8 weights and routed to four processing elements. By widening the memory interface while maintaining INT8 precision, the architecture delivers an effective fourfold increase in weight bandwidth. This strategy reduces the required memory accesses per computation cycle and alleviates the bandwidth limitations of conventional von Neumann architectures.

Multiple SNN arrays operate in parallel and connect via the AER interface, which broadcasts incoming spike events across the fabric. For hidden layers, internally generated spike events recirculate through the same interface to support time-multiplexed execution of subsequent layers on the same physical hardware.

Upon reaching the final layer, results stream to the decision unit. This unit uses a combinational comparator tree to identify the index of the maximum membrane potential while receiving 32-bit membrane potential data, enabling combinational readout in the same cycle without an additional pipeline stage.

\subsection{Barrier Synchronization Mechanism}

To maintain correct execution across parallel arrays without a shared chip-wide execution clock, each locally synchronous array integrates a lightweight barrier synchronizer.

The synchronizer uses a counter to track completion signals from active processing elements within the array. When a PE finishes its computation for the current event, it increments the counter. Once the counter value matches the number of active neurons scheduled for the current layer, the synchronizer asserts an array-level completion signal. This barrier ensures all partial membrane updates are completed before the next event is processed, preventing race conditions between concurrent computations. Operating locally within each array, this mechanism avoids treating completion as a chip-wide clocking signal.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}

We expanded the ablation study from SEED to SEED, DEAP, and DREAMER to test whether the roles of the principal components persist across datasets with different channel configurations and emotion-label structures. Five controlled conditions were evaluated under the subject-dependent random window-level 80/20 protocol: the complete DA-SNN, replacement of depthwise-separable convolution by standard convolution, removal of DSGM, replacement of DF-TTFS normalization by conventional floating-point min--max normalization, and replacement of the adaptive temporal window by fixed layer windows. Each condition used the same five random seeds (1--5). Table~\ref{tab:supp_ablation} reports the arithmetic mean and sample standard deviation (\texttt{ddof}=1) across these five runs.

The descriptive mean differences reveal both shared and dataset-dependent effects. Replacing the adaptive window with fixed windows lowers mean accuracy by 4.98, 10.75, and 0.83 percentage points on SEED, DEAP, and DREAMER, respectively. Removing DSGM lowers mean accuracy by 3.20, 17.58, and 0.37 points, while replacing depthwise-separable convolution lowers it by 1.87, 4.84, and 5.40 points. The smaller changes on DREAMER for DSGM and the adaptive window indicate that effect magnitude depends on the dataset rather than being uniform. Conventional min--max normalization changes mean accuracy by $+0.06$, $-1.07$, and $+0.66$ points relative to DF-TTFS on SEED, DEAP, and DREAMER, respectively. This mixed direction does not support a general accuracy advantage for DF-TTFS; its primary role is to replace floating-point division with power-of-two scaling for hardware-oriented implementation. These are descriptive comparisons of the reported means and are not presented as significance tests.
